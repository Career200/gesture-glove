Gesture Glove

A small, silent, eyes-free input interface worn on the hand.

A thin glove. The thumb is the controller; the other fingers carry contact zones on the knuckle creases, all findable by feel. Touching a zone is a signal, sliding across several is a compound one. No screen, no sound, and no gesture anyone else can see.

It's a design exploration — there's no hardware yet. The open question is what a silent, private, eyes-free channel is actually good for, and that's answerable long before anything gets soldered.

Reach trainer

The tool below measures how quickly and reliably a thumb finds each zone on a bare hand. It's what decided the current layout: it found that the middle segment of each finger is the one you can't reliably locate, because it's the only zone with no hard edge to stop against — and that the fingertip zones, predicted to be the worst, are among the best.

Running a session

  1. Mark your hand. One pen dot per finger segment, centred between the creases. The trainer shows the layout. Ink on skin is fine — the creases are the real landmarks.
  2. Run a baseline block first, once. Your thumb doesn't move in it at all — it just times how fast you react to a prompt, so that overhead can be subtracted from everything else. Skip it and the timings mean nothing.
  3. Keep a neutral position. Between trials, hand relaxed, thumb resting alongside the index, touching nothing. Without a fixed starting point the times aren't comparable.
  4. Say which hand wears the glove. It sets which response keys are shown — the keyboard is worked by the other hand — and it's recorded with your results, since left and right hands can't be pooled.
  5. Answer honestly. Space for a clean landing, K if you got there but had to hunt or drifted, M if you hit the wrong zone. Wearing the glove on the right instead? C and X do the same thing — both sets always work. The middle key matters most: sweeps rarely land wrong, they land approximately.
  6. Hide your hand. Look at the screen, not at your fingers. That's the condition the device actually has to work in.

If you'd like to send results

Everything stays in your browser unless you deliberately export it. Nothing is uploaded, there is no analytics and no tracking.

Use the export buttons to download a JSONL or CSV file, then send it along. The data is timings, zone names, and your own hit/sloppy/wrong marks — no personal information.

The written impressions are worth more than the numbers. The decision to drop the pinky came from someone saying it worked but didn't feel good — which no timing captured. If something is uncomfortable, imprecise, or awkward in a way the keys can't express, that's the valuable part. Hand size helps too, since it's likely the biggest difference between people.
